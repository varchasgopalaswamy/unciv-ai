# Optional Civ V strategy reference

These are suggestions, not instructions, rules, or a checklist. Choose from the
actual position and use them within your role. There is no prescribed build
order, city count or victory type; neither peace nor war is automatically best.
Use the current rules, available choices and native previews.

Check food, happiness, production, income and unit supply before discretionary
investments. Population needs useful tiles or specialist jobs; specialists take
citizens away from food and production. A unit-supply penalty is a real cost:
compare each unit's value with upgrades, gifts, disbanding or increasing supply.
Urgent defenders and saved Great People can justify temporary excess. Workers
need useful work, armies need attainable objectives, and wonders compete with
ordinary infrastructure. Reserves are valuable when they preserve useful options.

For an investment, compare its cost, time to usefulness, expected benefit and
what it delays. Include prerequisites and travel; straight-line distance is not
arrival time. Check what earlier choices actually achieved and reconsider plans
whose expectations fail.
Check the enabled turn limit when comparing plans.

Before accepting or renewing peace, compare who gains more and which opportunity
waiting could lose. Separate the costs of war from the additional costs of
breaking the promise. Private terms are not automatically public; consider who
could learn them. Social promises impose no automatic penalties or restrictions;
withdrawal is optional. Native treaties and friendship have their own effects.

Improving our output does not establish that we can finish before rivals.
Compare credible development, expansion, military and diplomatic alternatives.
An era suggests priorities without excluding an earlier victory opportunity.
In Vanilla, culture uses policy branches and Utopia, diplomacy needs the election
and votes, and domination follows the displayed capital objectives.

Messages should change a decision, agreement or useful understanding. Distinguish
observations, claims and assumptions. Avoid repeated unchanged reports; ask for
information when its answer could change your next choice.
