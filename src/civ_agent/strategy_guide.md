# Optional Civ V strategy reference

These are suggestions, not instructions, rules, or a checklist. You are free to
ignore or adapt any of them. Choose your own strategy from the actual position,
opponents, objectives, and available information. There is no preferred build
order, city count, diplomatic posture, or victory type to follow closely.
Different expansions, game speeds, difficulties, and opponents can
change which ideas pay off.

## 1. Find opportunities and build an opening around them

Early exploration can reveal settlement sites, neighbors, defensible approaches,
natural wonders, ruins, and city-states. An explorer has value because of what it
learns; losing it for one more revealed tile can be a poor bargain. Hills can
improve visibility. Terrain, rivers, and enemy zones of control affect travel, so
straight-line distance is a weak estimate of arrival time. A safe route can be
worth more than a short route, particularly for a settler or worker.

A promising city site combines useful food with enough production to become
productive. New luxury types, strategic resources, fresh water, coastal access,
and defensible terrain can all change its value. Consider the tiles that the
city can work soon, the improvements it needs, and the cost of getting there.
A distant resource can be attractive without making the intervening land safe.
Perfect-site hunting also has a cost: an unsettled settler produces nothing.

Expansion is an investment, not a prize for reaching a particular city count.
Additional cities can secure land and increase future output, but consume
happiness, require defense and improvements, and can delay national wonders.
Consider both the benefits and the next bottleneck. Pausing expansion may help
when the empire cannot absorb another city; remaining small indefinitely can
concede valuable land. A neighbor's objection is one strategic consideration,
not an automatic reason to surrender a worthwhile site.

Opening production choices solve different problems: exploration finds options,
workers improve yields, military protects investments, culture opens policies,
and settlers claim land. A remembered build order is useful only if its reasons
still apply. Liberty offers tools for expansion; Tradition often complements
investment in a strong capital and growing cities. Neither implies that every
game needs an early rush to a fixed number of settlements.

## 2. Keep growth, production, and the treasury working together

A higher settlement score is only one reason to change a Settler's destination.
Consider the extra travel and scouting turns, the value of founding sooner, and
what new information justifies waiting. Partly unseen surroundings do not by
themselves prevent a legal current-tile founding action. You may still prefer to
wait because of danger, happiness, or a better opportunity.

Population can support more worked tiles and science, but growth pays best when
there are useful tiles or specialist jobs available. A food-rich city with very
little production may struggle to build the infrastructure that makes its size
valuable. A production center can instead supply units, workers, or projects for
the empire. City roles can change as improved tiles and new buildings become
available; every city need not build the same infrastructure in the same order.

Workers are especially useful when improving tiles citizens actually work, or
connecting a resource needed soon. An impressive field of unused improvements
does not repay the same way. When choosing a building, consider its immediate
benefit, maintenance, and what its production time postpones. A library in a
growing science center may have a stronger case than a marginal building in a
tiny frontier town. Wonders compete with settlers, defense, and ordinary
infrastructure; losing a race can leave those needs unmet.

Happiness is a budget for growth and expansion. Connecting a new luxury type,
exchanging surplus luxuries, or adding an appropriate happiness building can
create room to grow. Duplicate resources may be valuable trade goods even when
they add no further happiness directly. Temporary growth restraint may help
with a shortage, but inspect what is causing it and how to restore productive
growth. Captures can create a sudden happiness problem, so conquest planning can
include occupation, annexation, puppeting, or razing choices where available.

City connections can earn income, but road tiles have upkeep. Compare the
likely connection income with route length and construction effort. A short
connection between developed cities may repay sooner than a long road to a
small outpost. Roads can also be justified by faster defense and reinforcement.
Harbors may connect suitable coastal cities; check the actual network and
blockade risks. A connection that looks profitable in peacetime may be fragile
when enemies can cut it.

Gold reserves create options: emergency purchases, upgrades, agreements, and
city-state influence. Positive income matters, but hoarding can also miss an
opportunity that would improve the position now. Trading surplus resources for
something useful can benefit both players. Evaluate duration, affordability,
future resource needs, and what the counterparty gains. Giving a rival strategic
materials or financing its expansion may be expensive even at a good price.

Science helps several victory paths by unlocking better yields and units.
Research can address a near-term need while moving toward a longer-term target.
Check the prerequisites and whether you can exploit the unlock: an expensive
military technology is less useful without production, resources, or upgrade
money. Libraries, universities, population, and specialists can reinforce one
another. Specialist assignments also take citizens away from tiles, so food
and production costs matter. Science-oriented policies such as Rationalism may
fit a developed research economy; adopting a branch for its name alone is not
a substitute for comparing the policies actually available.

National College timing can involve a tradeoff between early science and adding
cities whose libraries must be completed. Great People also present timing
choices: a lasting improvement has time to repay early, while an immediate
benefit can matter near a decisive deadline. Research agreements can
turn spare gold and a peaceful relationship into later science, but delayed
returns, cancellation through war, and benefits to a competitor matter.

## 3. Use diplomacy and military force for concrete purposes

During peace, consider whether existing defenders could cover your cities and
civilians if conditions changed. Scouts contribute reconnaissance but may offer
little defensive strength. New cities, lost units, and obsolete equipment can
change the tradeoff between another building and recruitment or upgrades. Visible
foreign troops are useful information without proving hostile intent; an empty
hostile-unit list does not establish that your defenses are sufficient.

Diplomacy can buy time, reduce the number of threatening fronts, exchange scarce
resources, or coordinate against a leader. Another player's request or claim is
evidence of what it wants you to believe, not proof that accepting helps you.
Consider incentives, credibility, enforcement, and alternatives. An agreement
that gives up a good city site might be worthwhile for a valuable concession,
but politeness alone is not a strategic benefit.

Social promises can be useful despite uncertain wording and imperfect trust.
Clarifying terms may prevent conflict, or expose a genuine disagreement. You
can keep, renegotiate, refuse, or break a promise if that best serves your goals;
consider likely reactions and future cooperation. Those consequences depend on
the players and native mechanics, not an invented obligation to comply. Built-in
AI diplomatic behavior should not be assumed to predict human or agent choices.

City-states can provide resources and other benefits, and may matter for a
diplomatic victory. Influence is an ongoing competition, not permanent ownership.
Quests can be good opportunities when achievable without sacrificing a more
valuable objective. Compare a gift or alliance with the gold's other uses and
the duration of benefits. Concentrating investment on a few useful relationships
may be better than scattered gifts that establish none. Policies supporting
city-state relations make more sense when those relationships are central to
the plan.

A war is easier to evaluate with a concrete objective: defend a city, seize a
resource, remove an immediate threat, or take a capital. Compare available forces
and technology with terrain, reinforcements, happiness, treasury, and the risk
of another front. A stronger army on paper can still be spread too thin. Native
diplomatic consequences of aggression can affect players beyond the target.
Peace may be valuable once the attainable objective is secured or the costs
outgrow it.

Combat strength, terrain, fortification, health, and promotions can change a
fight substantially. A favorable attack preview does not show every enemy's
next move. Consider counterattacks and retreat space before committing an
important unit. Preserving an experienced unit may be worth delaying an attack;
an immediate sacrifice can also be justified by a decisive gain. Reinforcements
and upgrades often matter more than chasing a damaged enemy into exposure.

Ranged units can deal damage behind a protective screen, while exposed ranged
and siege units may be vulnerable in close combat. Sight, range, and suitable
firing positions can matter as much as raw numbers. Concentrated attacks can
remove a threat before it acts again. When attacking cities, retain a unit that
can actually occupy the city after bombardment. Siege support needs protection
and time to reach a firing position.

Water changes logistics. Embarked troops may need escorts and a safe landing
area. A fleet is more useful when it supports a concrete naval objective than
when ships sit far from the relevant coast. In later eras, air range, basing,
interception, and replenishment can similarly determine whether superior units
can influence the battle. Inspect the capabilities of the units actually
available; names and assumptions from another ruleset can mislead.

## 4. Turn a good position into a win

A growing empire still needs a route to victory. Periodically compare the
remaining requirements, expected time, and visible rival progress. Keeping a
second option can help, but pursuing every wonder and victory path can delay
all of them. The leading player may need protection and efficient completion;
a trailing player may need a calculated risk or coalition.

For the Vanilla rules used here, science victory involves Apollo and the
required spaceship parts, including delivering and adding the parts in the
capital. Research and production alone do not finish assembly. Cultural victory
uses completed policy branches and the Utopia Project, rather than tourism.
Diplomatic victory requires the relevant election and enough votes; influence
well before the vote may not remain sufficient at election time. Domination
depends on the game's displayed capital objectives, not merely destroying many
units. For a time victory, score matters when the turn limit arrives. Verify the
current victory requirements and enabled types before committing resources.

An effective strategic review can be brief: what improved or worsened, what is
the current bottleneck, and which next investment best advances a plausible win?
Change course when evidence changes. These suggestions are options to think
with, not milestones you need to satisfy.
